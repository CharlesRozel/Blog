### Font Licensing for Authors

Below is a chapter from my ePub called _Font Licensing for Authors__ available on Amazon.

The US is one of the few countries that does not protect typeface design under copyright law. To understand why this is so we need to look at The Copyright Act of 1976, which is still current law in the US, and extends copyright protection to pictorial, graphic and sculptural works. Such works shall include works of artistic craftsmanship insofar as their form but not their mechanical or utilitarian aspects are concerned.
  
  The word _utilitarian_ is the clue to why typeface design is not copyrightable in the US. A design that is also a useful article is considered _a pictorial, graphic, or sculptural work only to the extent that such design incorporates pictorial, graphic, or sculptural features that can be identified separately from and are capable of existing independently of the utilitarian aspects of the article._

  Typefaces and fonts are inherently utilitarian; they comprises letters or glyphs which form words.

  An article is considered useful if it has an intrinsic utilitarian function. If you stretch a point and consider a typeface to be an article it is a useful article.

  The function of a font is to produce text which can be read. The reader will notice if the the font is Garamond or Palatino.

  In 1979, the judgment in the case of Eltra Corp. v. Ringer was that typefaces are industrial designs which cannot exist independently as works of art.
 
  The Copyright Act in 1976 and the Eltra case predate the arrival of desktop publishing in the eighties.

  In 1988 the US Copyright Office confirmed that the design of a computerised typeface cannot be copyrighted.

  However, in 1992 the Copyright Office said in a statement that programs designed for generating a typeface may involve original computer instructions entitled to protection under the Copyright Act: meaning that it was the software program which produced the font which could be copyright protected; not the typeface itself. Whether the software program could be protected depended on the degree of originality of the code written by the programmer.
 
  In 1988 fonts were bitmapped (think of a rasterised photograph) whereas, by 1992, fonts were becoming scalable (think of vectors and Bézier curves) and there was more scope for originality in the code that produced a font.
 
  The case of Adobe v. Southern Software, in 1998, held that the choice of control points (Bézier curves etc) by the programmer could be classified.
 
  Font foundries and font-owners relied on the Adobe case to argue that font software could be copyrighted.
 
  US copyright law distinguishes between typeface design and font software and does not say that typeface design is not copyrightable. As mentioned above, font software may be protected by copyright if the computer program that generates the typeface design is original enough.
  
  From about 2018, the US Copyright Office began to reject applications to copyright fonts.These rejections were considered in the 2023 case of Laatz v Zazzle. My understanding is that this was the first US court case which analysed the finding in the seminal case of Adobe v. Southern Software mentioned above.
  
  A British font designer named Nicky Laatz claimed that Zazzle, an online market place in the US, had used her fonts without a licence. Laatz relied on the judgement in the Adobe case which said that choice of control points, curves etc, in the font software by the programer could be classified as an original work and could be copyrighted. However, the court's judgement was that the Laatz case differed from the Adobe case because Adobe's fonts were created using Adobe-created software, whereas Laatz did not create the software which made her fonts.
  
  Some argue that this judgment means that typefaces (fonts) cannot be copyrighted in the US.
 
  Monotype argues that that fonts can be copyrighted, and that the Adobe case is not different from the Laatz case because there is no distinction in the Copyright Act between proprietary (Adobe's) and third party (Laatz's) software.
  
  Point to note: the judgement in the Laatz case is not binding on other cases involving the same question as to whether typefaces can be copyrighted.
